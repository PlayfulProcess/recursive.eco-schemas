# An Ethics of Fiction — research dossier

*Companion to [`WHAT_IS_COMPOST.md`](../WHAT_IS_COMPOST.md). Compost says: "Not a ban list —
everything here can be read. The point is HOW you read it." This dossier asks the question that
sentence leaves open:* **can an eight-year-old read that way?** *If the answer is no, then
"compost it in class" is a promise the classroom cannot keep, and the honest move is to change
what we hand them — not to ban what we don't.*

**Status:** research spine, not an essay. Confidence flags on every claim.
`[SOLID]` = I'd publish it. `[CHECK]` = right shape, verify wording/date/finding before print.
`[CONTESTED]` = live scholarly fight — say so in the essay, don't pretend it's settled.

## 1. The anchor: Wayne Booth and Paul Moses

Wayne Booth, *The Company We Keep: An Ethics of Fiction* (California, 1988).

`[CHECK]` Booth recounts a University of Chicago curriculum meeting (early 1960s) where **Paul
Moses**, a Black colleague, argued that *Huckleberry Finn* should not be taught — that it was
degrading, and that he could not stand in front of students and teach it. Booth and the other
white faculty treated the objection as a *category error*: an unsophisticated reader mistaking
ethics for aesthetics. Booth returns to the episode decades later as the founding scandal of his
book — not because Moses was right about Twain, but because the profession **had no vocabulary in
which Moses could be wrong or right.** Ethical criticism had been ruled out of bounds, so the
objection couldn't be heard at all.

**Verify before printing:** Moses's exact words (Booth quotes him, and the quote is strong — do not
paraphrase from memory), the year, and the page. Booth's own later position on Twain is
*not* "ban it" — get that right or the essay's opponents will get it right for you.

**Why it's the anchor.** It is the same argument, sixty years earlier, in a country that had it
first. And it ends the way you want yours to end: not in prohibition, but in a man admitting that
the reason he couldn't hear the objection was that his theory of literature was built to make it
inaudible.

### Booth's two portable tools

- `[SOLID]` **"The company we keep."** A book is time spent in someone's company. The question is
  not "is this well made" but "who does this make me, over the hours I spend with it, and would I
  choose this friend for my child."
- `[SOLID]` **Coduction** (Booth's coinage). Literary value isn't deduced from principles or
  induced from data — it's arrived at *comparatively and communally*: I tell you how this book
  worked on me, you tell me how it worked on you, and the judgment lives in that exchange.
  **This is the whole answer to "who are you to say the canon isn't the best."** Nobody measured.
  It was always coduction — just with a very small, very European jury. Widen the jury and the
  verdict moves. That isn't relativism; it's the method finally being run honestly.

## 2. The empirical spine — can a child critically resist a story they love?

The claim to defend is *not* "reading Lobato makes kids racist." No such study exists; staking the
argument there loses it. The defensible claim is narrower and harder to answer:

> **The "we'll read it critically" plan assumes a cognitive capacity that children under ~11 do
> not reliably have, and assumes it works against absorption — when the research says absorption
> is exactly what suppresses it.**

### 2.1 Children can't deploy the defense we're assuming

`[SOLID]` The advertising-and-children literature is the cleanest evidence, and it's about
30-second ads — shorter, cruder, and far less immersive than a novel. Children below roughly 7–8
don't reliably recognise *persuasive intent* at all; older children recognise it but don't
spontaneously *use* it as a defense. The key finding: **conceptual knowledge does not
automatically become attitudinal resistance.** A child can tell you the ad wants their money and
still want the cereal.

→ Sources: APA Task Force on Advertising and Children (Kunkel et al., 2004); Deborah Roedder John,
"Consumer Socialization of Children," *J. Consumer Research* (1999); Rozendaal et al. on
advertising literacy. `[CHECK]` exact age thresholds — they vary by measure and by study.

**The move for the essay:** if an eight-year-old can't counterargue a cereal commercial, what
exactly do we think they're doing to Emília?

### 2.2 The better the book, the weaker the defense

`[SOLID]` **Transportation theory** — Green & Brock, "The Role of Transportation in the
Persuasiveness of Public Narratives," *JPSP* (2000). The more absorbed a reader is in a narrative,
the *less* they counterargue. Absorption and critical evaluation are inversely related.

**This is the trap, and it should be the essay's hinge:** Lobato is a genuinely great writer.
The craft is not a mitigating factor. **The craft is the delivery mechanism.**

### 2.3 The route is identification — and Lobato aimed it

`[SOLID]` Narrative persuasion runs through identification with characters (Cohen, "Defining
Identification," *Mass Communication & Society*, 2001).

`[CHECK]` **Vezzali, Stathi, Giovannini, Capozza & Trifiletti (2015), "The greatest magic of Harry
Potter: Reducing prejudice," *J. Applied Social Psychology*.** Reading Potter passages reduced
prejudice toward stigmatised groups — **but the effect depended on identifying with Harry rather
than with Voldemort, and on guided discussion.**

This study is the essay's best card, because it cuts both ways and you should say so:

1. Fiction demonstrably moves children's real-world prejudice. Nobody gets to call this soft.
2. The mechanism is identification with the character the child loves.
3. In the Sítio, the character children are engineered to love — funny, rebellious, talks back —
   is **Emília**, and Emília is the one who says *"coisa mesmo de negra beiçuda"* and
   *"eu cortava um pedaço desse beiço."* Dona Benta scolds her for disrespecting elders. Never for
   the slur.

Lobato built the identification vehicle and loaded it. That's not a period accident.

### 2.4 Children do not extract the anti-racist moral on their own

`[CHECK]` Rebecca Bigler's work is the direct hit: multicultural materials and counter-stereotypic
content do **not** reliably produce anti-bias outcomes without *explicit* adult framing, and can
backfire. See Bigler (1999), "The use of multicultural curricula and materials to counter racism in
children," *J. Social Issues*; Hughes, Bigler & Levy (2007) on explicit vs. implicit racial
messages in children's history learning.

`[SOLID]` **Developmental intergroup theory** (Bigler & Liben, 2006/2007): children build
stereotypes when an environment makes a social category *perceptually salient* and *correlates it
with status* — with nobody uttering a prejudiced word. The Sítio does this structurally: one Black
adult; she is the servant; illiterate; afraid; compared to animals; her stories ranked below
Europe's by the narrative's own moral authority. **The slurs only remove the ambiguity.**

**Corollary that wins the argument about schools:** the "we'll discuss it critically" plan requires
teacher skill, willingness, and time that is not evenly distributed — and where it's absent, the
research says the reading does harm, not nothing.

### 2.5 Affect doesn't wait for cognition

`[SOLID]` Zajonc, "Feeling and Thinking: Preferences Need No Inferences," *American Psychologist*
(1980). Evaluative responses form fast, without inference, and precede the verbal account of them.

`[CONTESTED]` The stronger version — that evaluative conditioning is unaware and resists verbal
correction — is a live fight. State it as a direction of evidence, not a law.

**This is your intuition, stated in their language:** "fiction cuts through the subconscious" is
the folk phrasing of a real asymmetry. You cannot reliably talk a child out of an affect they
acquired by loving someone.

### 2.6 The novela evidence — and it's Brazilian

`[CHECK]` **La Ferrara, Chong & Duryea, "Soap Operas and Fertility: Evidence from Brazil,"
*AEJ: Applied Economics* (2012).** Staggered arrival of Rede Globo across Brazilian municipalities
causally reduced fertility, with effects tracking the family structures depicted in the novelas.
Companion work by Chong & La Ferrara on divorce.

Verify the finding's shape before citing, but if it holds it is extraordinary for this essay:
**a top-journal causal demonstration, in Brazil, that fiction restructures the most intimate real
decisions of adults** — adults, with fully formed critical faculties, who all knew it was fiction.
Then ask what it does to an eight-year-old who doesn't yet know that knowing doesn't help.

`[SOLID]` Background frame: Gerbner's cultivation theory — heavy narrative exposure shifts beliefs
about social reality.

### 2.7 Where the harm actually lands

No study needed, and this is the paragraph that ends the argument:

> A Black eight-year-old in a class where the teacher reads *"macaca de carvão"* aloud pays a cost
> **in that room, that morning, in front of thirty children who now have a phrase.** Ask who does
> the composting labour in that classroom. It isn't the teacher.

`[SOLID]` Background: Clark & Clark doll studies (1947) and their modern replications — the cost of
a racial hierarchy in the culture's stories is paid first as self-concept by the children inside
the category.

## 3. "High taste" is a class weapon, not a measurement

⚠️ **Attribution correction — do not print "Todorov" here.** The argument you want is:

- `[SOLID]` **Norbert Elias, *The Civilizing Process* (1939).** Courtly etiquette and refined
  manners functioned as *distinction machinery* by which the nobility held status precisely as it
  lost military and political power to the bourgeoisie and the absolutist state. Manners were what
  they had left, so manners became the border.
- `[SOLID]` **Pierre Bourdieu, *Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste* (1979).**
  The empirical demolition: "high taste" tracks class origin and educational capital, not any
  intrinsic property of the object. Taste is a mechanism for converting inherited position into
  apparent merit.

**Todorov is still useful, just for a different job:** *The Conquest of America* (1982) on the
semiotics of the colonial encounter, and *On Human Diversity* (1989) on ethnocentrism that presents
itself as universalism — which is exactly the operation when a European children's canon is called
"the classics" and everything else is called "folklore."

**The synthesis with Booth:** if value was always established by coduction — comparative,
communal judgment — then "the classics" is not a measurement result. It's the verdict of a small
jury that excluded most of the world and then called its own verdict universal. `[SOLID]`

**The claim you can make, and should hedge exactly this much:** there is no evidence base
establishing the European children's canon as developmentally superior. `[CHECK]` — before
printing, search for anyone who has actually tried to test it; the absence is the finding, but
you must look before claiming absence.

## 4. Infancy is originary, not modern

Your claim is contrarian in a way the literature already supports. This is a real fight and you're
on the winning side of it now.

- `[SOLID]` **Philippe Ariès, *Centuries of Childhood* (1960)** — the famous thesis that childhood
  was *invented* in the modern era; medieval society had no concept of it.
- `[SOLID]` **The demolition:** Linda Pollock, *Forgotten Children* (1983) — diaries and letters
  show parental grief, tenderness and age-differentiation throughout the period Ariès said had
  none; Shulamith Shahar, *Childhood in the Middle Ages* (1990). Ariès's evidence base (art
  conventions, elite sources) doesn't carry the claim.
- `[SOLID]` **Van Gennep, *Les rites de passage* (1909)**; **Victor Turner, *The Ritual Process*
  (1969)** on liminality. Age-grading and elaborate initiation are near-ubiquitous in the
  ethnographic record — which is the direct refutation: **societies without a concept of childhood
  do not build machinery for leaving it.**

**The reframe, which is the essay's best idea:** modernity did not *discover* childhood. Industrial
labour destroyed the passage structures that already marked it, and then modernity sold childhood
back as a *protected consumer enclosure* — sanitised, extended, and stripped of the rites that gave
it an exit. **We solved a problem industrialisation created and took credit for the concept.**

`[CONTESTED]` Be careful not to romanticise: initiation practices include ones that are genuinely
violent, and "originary peoples" is 5,000 distinct societies, not one position. Name specific
traditions or say "many," never "indigenous people believe."

## 5. Fictions of death and sex

`[SOLID]` **Jack Zipes** — already the decomposer in your Grimm compost row. The precise finding is
better than the general one: across the seven Grimm editions the brothers **sanitised sex while
intensifying punishment** — Rapunzel's pregnancy removed, mothers turned into stepmothers, and the
tortures made more elaborate. Modernity didn't soften children's stories. It moved which
unspeakable thing was unspeakable.

`[CONTESTED]` **Bruno Bettelheim, *The Uses of Enchantment* (1976)** — that violence in fairy tales
is psychologically necessary for children. Cite with the caveat: the Freudian apparatus is not
credible and there are substantiated plagiarism and credential allegations against Bettelheim. Use
**Maria Tatar, *Off With Their Heads!* (1992)** as the citable version of the same territory.

**The research question this opens (§8, Q4):** if a culture forbids children fictions of death and
sex, but hands them fictions of racial hierarchy without comment — what has it actually decided is
dangerous? That inversion is the essay's sharpest single observation. Follow it.

## 6. The cathedral

Your metaphor, and it's the structural spine — it resolves the objection that will otherwise sink
the essay ("but Moana and Frozen are Disney colonialism too" — yes, and you already have them in
compost as *Roots of Moana* / *Roots of Frozen II*).

`[SOLID]` Chartres, Notre-Dame, the Mezquita-Catedral of Córdoba: Romanesque core, Gothic nave,
Baroque chapel, twentieth-century glass. Nobody calls Chartres ruined by its later windows.

`[SOLID]` **The Viollet-le-Duc card, which is almost too good.** The spire the world mourned when
Notre-Dame burned in 2019 was **not medieval.** It was Eugène Viollet-le-Duc's nineteenth-century
addition, built during a "restoration" that invented as much as it recovered — and the 2019–2024
debate over whether to rebuild it or add contemporary work was a fight about whether to preserve a
previous generation's *intervention* as though it were the original.

> **The "authentic original" people defend is almost always itself a later intervention.**

Same for Lobato: the text being defended has already passed through editions, illustrators, and
2010s sanitised reprints. There is no untouched original to protect. The only question was ever
*who is allowed to build next.*

**And the real scandal, stated:** it isn't that Lobato is flawed. Everything is flawed; that's what
compost is for. It's that he is treated as **finished** — as a monument rather than a foundation
course.

## 7. Why orality respects the builder

`[SOLID]` **Walter Ong, *Orality and Literacy* (1982).** In oral transmission every telling is a
revision fitted to this room, this night, these children. There is no fixed text to be unfaithful
to. Writing freezes the utterance and — with print, copyright and authorship — converts a living
practice into **property with an owner and a correct version.**

`[SOLID]` **Thomas King, *The Truth About Stories* (2003, Massey Lectures).** The best single
source for a general audience on story as relationship and obligation rather than artefact.

`[SOLID]` **Robin Wall Kimmerer, *Braiding Sweetgrass*** — stories as instructions carried forward,
not objects preserved.

**The link back:** a cathedral is architecture that behaves like an oral tradition — each
generation permitted to speak. A canonised children's book is architecture that has been told it
must never be spoken again. Your instinct is right, and the mechanism is *authorship as property*,
which is exactly what Lobato does to Nastácia inside the book: she transmits, she does not author.
**The form of the injury and the form of the defense of the book are the same form.**

## 8. Open research questions

1. **Has anyone tested it?** Is there *any* study comparing children's outcomes across
   canonical-European vs. locally-authored / counter-canonical children's literature? My prior:
   no direct test exists. If that's right, the absence is itself publishable — but look first.
2. **Age of critical reception.** At what age can a child reliably (a) notice that a beloved
   narrator holds a view, (b) disagree with it, and (c) *retain* the disagreement a week later?
   (c) is the one nobody measures, and it's the one the classroom plan depends on.
3. **Brazilian classroom evidence.** Is there any observational study of what actually happens in a
   Brazilian classroom when *Caçadas de Pedrinho* is read aloud — who speaks, who goes quiet, what
   the Black children report afterward? If it doesn't exist, that's the study to ask someone to do.
4. **The inversion.** Map what a given culture forbids children in fiction (death, sex, violence)
   against what it hands them without comment (racial hierarchy, servitude as natural). Does the
   ratio predict anything? This is a genuinely original question and it may be the essay's core.
5. **Does a PG-style rating for *social* content exist anywhere?** Ratings index violence, sex and
   language. Nothing indexes "this narrative naturalises a racial hierarchy and the point-of-view
   character endorses it." Why not — and who would have to build it?
6. **Ariès's afterlife.** Trace how the "childhood is modern" thesis, largely discredited by
   historians, became settled common sense in education and policy. That's a compost story about
   an academic text, and it belongs in the pile.
7. **The Emília problem, generalised.** How often, across beloved children's literature, is the
   bigotry placed in the mouth of the *most charismatic* character and left uncorrected by the
   narration? A survey would be a finding.

## 10. Facing your own — the mirror

`[SOLID]` **Project Implicit** (Harvard) — free, in browser: https://implicit.harvard.edu/implicit/

Brazil/Portuguese portal: https://implicit.harvard.edu/implicit/brazil/

Relevant tests: Race (Black–White), Skin-tone, **Sexuality (Gay–Straight)** — the one you took —
Arab-Muslim, Weight, Age, Gender–Career.

`[CONTESTED]` **State the caveat yourself before a critic does.** The IAT's test–retest reliability
is modest, and its power to predict an individual's behaviour is genuinely disputed — see Oswald et
al. (2013) meta-analysis, and Forscher et al. (2019), which found that shifting implicit measures
does **not** reliably shift behaviour.

Which is exactly why your use of it is the legitimate one: **not as a verdict, as a mirror.** You
took it, you found something you didn't like, you said so in public. That is a report of an
encounter, not a measurement claim — and it is unfalsifiable in the good way. Nobody can tell you
that you didn't have it.

`[SOLID]` The honest framing to write toward, and the essay's ending: *if I carry this, and I have
spent years on it deliberately, then the eight-year-old is not the one who should be assigned the
critical labour.*
